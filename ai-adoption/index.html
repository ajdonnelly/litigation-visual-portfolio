AI adoption · a body of work inside a litigation firm

This is AI work I have been doing inside a litigation firm.

Most recently I have been one of the people helping my firm adopt generative AI. That has meant testing tools, building guardrails, coordinating with stakeholders across the firm, and creating the training and materials that help colleagues actually take it up.

It turned out to be very close to what a litigation creative and visual-strategy team does. The through-line is the same: coordinate with the people who own the work, get hands-on with the technology, and build deliverables that help a specific audience take in complex information and learn from it. That is the overlap I would bring to this role.

About

I have already been a legal team’s AI-adoption person

Most recently, as a senior litigation paralegal, I served on my firm’s AI committee and helped run an enterprise pilot. I integrated tools like Microsoft Copilot and Filevine’s LOIS into real legal work, tested them against live tasks, evaluated the output for reliability, documented workflows, and helped train colleagues at every level.

That work made me fluent in the part that matters most in a legal setting, which is responsible use. I closely checked AI-generated medical chronologies against the source records. It was concrete practice in human review, catching hallucinations, and protecting confidential and privileged data.

For more than a decade before that I ran the daily operations of a boutique firm from end to end, through repeated waves of new technology. I handled billing and case-management migrations, digitized files, and got busy legal professionals to trust new systems. My graduate training in biomedical visualization is about making complex information understandable, and this fall I begin a part-time, evening J.D. at the University of Hawaiʻi focused on AI law and governance.

That background shapes how I design adoption. Biomedical visualization is really applied instructional and communication design. It is the work of taking complex, technical material and making it usable for a specific audience. I bring the same discipline to legal AI. I lean on adult learning theory to build enablement that actually sticks, with role-based pathways, training that adds complexity only as fast as confidence grows, and steady practice and support rather than a single session people forget. Adoption is a learning problem, and learning problems have a design discipline.

This work has taught me as much as it has taught anyone else. You learn something best when you have to teach it, and working out AI workflows and interventions for other people at the firm level has sharpened how I think about my own. Some of my best ideas for my personal workflows started as ways to help a colleague with theirs.

The approach

Responsible adoption, by design

Getting a legal team to use AI is not a tool rollout. It is behavior change, earned safely. Four principles guide the work, and the last one is non-negotiable in legal work.

01

Meet the work

Learn how each practice group and business team actually works before recommending where AI fits.

02

Enable every role

Role-based pathways for attorneys, associates, paralegals, and business professionals, because each adopts differently.

03

Guardrails first

Confidentiality, data classification, human-in-the-loop review, and citing sources, built into every workflow, not bolted on.

04

Measure value and risk

Track adoption and time saved, and watch for misuse, bias, and hallucination patterns, escalating risk signals early.

How I’d run it

A bounded responsible-AI adoption pilot

Rather than a firmwide push, I’d start with one practice group and one high-value workflow, prove it safely, and expand on evidence, mapping the real work first, then enabling each role with guardrails built in.

Start from the real workflow

Map how a practice group actually works before configuring anything, so AI lands where it helps and where oversight matters.

  1. Intake and issue framing
  2. Research and analysis
  3. Drafting and review
  4. Knowledge capture and reuse
  5. Client delivery

Enable safely, stage by stage

The work that takes a group from curious to confident, with confidentiality and human review throughout.

01

Vision & guardrails

Leadership alignment, approved-tool scope, and the confidentiality and data-handling rules up front.

02

Workshops & onboarding

Role-based, hands-on sessions: prompt technique, verifying outputs, citing sources, and when to escalate.

03

Champions & comms

Identify champions to share safe success stories and reinforce standards through peer learning.

04

Metrics & risk signals

Adoption, time saved, and quality, plus misuse and hallucination signals routed to the right teams.

Experience

I have done this work inside a firm

Strategy & governance

A 90-day AI pilot I co-built and presented

With a colleague on my firm’s AI team, I co-created and presented a 90-day AI pilot program for our office, the strategic rationale, governance principles (low-risk, human-reviewed, measurable, iterative, secure), an explicit included/excluded scope, an approved tech stack (Filevine + Claude), a phased rollout, and success metrics covering value and risk. It is real internal firm work, so I keep it confidential and can share it directly on request. The samples below show how I’d take that pilot’s governance foundation and build it into full, role-based enablement.

Workflow & change management

From workflow assessment to change management

On the same AI committee and enterprise pilot, I helped design the rollout alongside Filevine’s change managers and assessed paralegal workflows, deliberately identifying a focused set of high-value, automatable use cases to target, including a real pain point for us in med mal: medical chronologies.

It is the same instinct behind the visual work: find where people get stuck with something complex, then build the thing that gets them unstuck. Coordinating with stakeholders, working directly in the technology, and producing materials people can actually learn from is the overlap I would carry into a litigation creative and visual-strategy role.

The analysis behind the verification habit

Evaluating an AI-assisted medical chronology workflow

Showcase · Selected work

The responsible-AI enablement suite

These aren’t five standalone samples, they’re one cohesive system, built the way I’d run enablement on the job. Each piece meets people in a different modality and at a different altitude, so complexity rises only as fast as confidence does.

The blueprint deck is the first engagement: the 40,000-foot view that frames the pilot for participants. From there, specificity sharpens: the interactive module teaches the actual habits, one node in a larger learning interface. The tracker puts a tool in the champion’s hands to log what’s working, and that data aggregates upward to inform firm strategy. The podcast reaches the people who’d rather listen than read. And the playbook is the management-facing 90-day plan, setting out the qualities and results responsible adoption should produce. Different modes, different audiences, one through-line, built to work within a firm’s governance, not to replace it.

Why this belongs in the portfolio

How this maps to the role

This looks like a detour from illustration and demonstratives, but it is the same job in a different medium.

A litigation creative and visual-strategy team lives on three things: coordinating closely with the attorneys and stakeholders who own the work, being comfortable and hands-on with the technology that produces the deliverable, and building materials that help a specific audience take in complex information and act on it. Helping my firm adopt AI was exactly that. I worked across practice groups and with the people responsible for the tools, I got into the technology myself rather than managing it from a distance, and everything I made was aimed at getting complex material to land with a real audience.

So I include it here on purpose. It shows the coordination, the technical fluency, and the information-and-learning design that this role asks for, proven on live work where the stakes and the confidentiality were real. It is the same discipline as the visual work, pointed at a different kind of problem.
